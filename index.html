How to tune it

  1. Sing (or play the demo) a steady vowel and press Freeze to hold a few cycles.
  2. Press Auto estimate for a starting point.
  3. Look at the closed phase in the flow. If it ripples about once per cycle of fR1, move fR1. Faster ripples point to fR2 and above.
  4. Widen or narrow each one until the ripple is gone. In the spectrum, the orange trace should lose its peaks and fall off smoothly.
  5. If the closed phase tilts or sags instead of rippling, that's usually the microphone, not the voice. Try the low-frequency correction.

Inverse filtering is most reliable on lower, steady pitches with the mic close to the mouth. Above roughly C5, the harmonics are so far apart that fR1 is hard to pin down, by hand or by computer. That limitation is real, not a bug.

Input

A steady sustained vowel works best: WAV, MP3, M4A or AIFF.

Use headphones or turn speakers down, and sing about a hand's width from the mic. The browser's echo cancelling, noise suppression and auto level are switched off for this, since they change the waveform.

On an iPhone or iPad, switch the ringer on (off silent) to hear the demo.

At this pitch, fR1 and fR2 can't be measured reliably, by hand or by Auto. The harmonics are too far apart to show where the resonances are, so the estimate tends to land on a harmonic instead.

Off

Most mics and interfaces roll off the lowest frequencies, which makes the closed phase tilt or sag. Raise this until the closed phase is level. Leave it off for the demo.

4000 Hz

Inverse filtering boosts everything above the resonances, including hiss and room noise. This cuts the result off above the chosen frequency. Lower it if the flow is covered in fast fuzz. On a real voice, Auto sets this to 3500 Hz (or a little above fR4 if fR4 is high), since nothing above that is being removed.

About

Inverse Filter Lab was made by Chadley Ballantyne (Stetson University) as a companion to the Vowel Synth Lab, in the tradition of Sopran and the interactive inverse filtering pioneered by Svante Granqvist. Each resonance is removed with a second-order anti-resonance filter, and the result is integrated to estimate the glottal flow. The automatic estimate uses closed-phase covariance analysis: it finds each glottal closure, fits a linear predictor only to the stretches of each cycle when the folds are closed and the tract is ringing on its own, tries several closed-phase windows, and keeps the one that leaves the flattest closed phase. If no clear closures can be found, it falls back to linear prediction after a first-pass removal of the glottal tilt (a simplified IAIF) and a search that flattens the closed phase. The demo voice is the same Liljencrants-Fant source and five resonances as the Vowel Synth Lab. Built with Claude.
